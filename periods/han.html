The Dynasty That Became a People

Four centuries of compromise, reform and frontier government, and a name that outlived every institution built to carry it.

Written and edited by one writer · Last reviewed November 2026 · Sources listed at the end

The Han dynasty is the point at which the imperial system stopped being an experiment and became a habit. It began with a compromise among men who had armies, survived a civil war, and ended with an empire whose census counted nearly sixty million people. Its name became the word Chinese people use for themselves.

A capital was chosen before a constitution was written

In 202 BC Liu Bang, a former local official who had won the war against Qin and then against his rivals, took the title of emperor. He chose Chang'an, south of the Wei River and west of the old Qin capital, and the court began a walled city that would be enlarged for two centuries.

The government was a hybrid. About half the empire was divided into commanderies under appointed officials, following Qin practice; the other half was granted as kingdoms to relatives and to generals whose support had been bought. The arrangement, jun guo bing xing, was less a theory than a settlement among armed men. Taxes were cut, labour service reduced, and the court adopted a policy of rest later described as wu wei, non-action: a population exhausted by war could not pay.

The first emperors ruled half an empire directly

For about seventy years the court governed its own commanderies and left the kingdoms largely alone. The result was peace, a growing population and a court that spent less than it collected. The reigns of Emperor Wen and Emperor Jing, from 180 to 141 BC, are grouped as the Rule of Wen and Jing, a model of low taxation.

Land tax was reduced to one thirtieth of the harvest and labour service was shortened, while the currency stayed stable and grain filled state granaries. The surplus was real, and a later, more aggressive emperor would spend it. The weakness was structural: a kingdom with its own army and treasury is a state inside a state, and peace made the kingdoms richer.

A rebellion taught the court to shrink the kingdoms

In 154 BC seven kingdoms rose against Emperor Jing, led by the king of Wu, after the court began reducing their territories. The revolt was suppressed within months but settled the question: the kingdoms were broken up, their officials replaced by imperial appointees, their armies dissolved.

The next instrument was administrative. From 127 BC the beneficence decree required each kingdom to divide its land among all the king's sons instead of passing it whole to the eldest. A kingdom with five heirs became five small principalities, each too weak to matter. By the end of the second century BC the kingdoms were ceremonial and the commandery and county governed everywhere: the compromise of 202 BC reversed without a second civil war, by a rule about inheritance.

The throne began to recruit officials by reputation

The Han needed officials loyal to the court rather than to a family, and appointment from the top alone produced a closed service. In 134 BC Emperor Wu ordered each commandery and kingdom to recommend one man for filial piety and one for integrity, followed by examination at court. This is the origin of the cha ju system.

The criteria were moral as much as technical: a candidate was known in his district for conduct toward parents and honesty in office, and a sponsor answered for a bad recommendation. The quota grew, and the road into office ran through recommendation and the imperial academy established in 124 BC. It widened recruitment beyond the old military families, but recommenders favoured the sons of people they knew, and by the Eastern Han the route had become partly hereditary.

The state took the salt and the iron

Emperor Wu's wars against the Xiongnu were expensive, and the treasury Wen and Jing had filled was not enough. From 119 BC the court began to bring salt and iron under state control, and by 117 BC the monopoly was formal: producers worked under official supervision and sold only to the state, while iron ore, smelting and tool sales were managed by government offices.

Iron made weapons and farm tools, salt was bought by every household, and a charge on both could be collected without a new land survey. The court added state management of grain transport and prices, and in 118 BC issued the wu zhu coin. Critics at a court debate in 81 BC said the state workshops made poor tools and officials competed with merchants; the monopoly was relaxed and reinstated. What survived was the principle that the court managed critical commodities.

A failed alliance produced a managed corridor

In 138 BC Emperor Wu sent Zhang Qian west with about a hundred men to find the Yuezhi, displaced by the Xiongnu, and propose an alliance. He was captured, held for years, escaped, reached the Yuezhi, found them unwilling to fight, and returned in 126 BC with one companion left of his party.

He brought back a description of the Tarim Basin states, their markets, armies and rivalries. A second mission in 119 BC went with a larger party as far as Bactria, and the Han took the Hexi Corridor and set up four commanderies, Wuwei, Zhangye, Jiuquan and Dunhuang, between 121 and 111 BC. The corridor was managed: soldier farmers, garrison granaries, passes that collected duties and checked documents, post stations with horses. Excavated frontier documents show leave permits, ration accounts and passport checks.

Confucian texts became the language of office

The Han did not abolish the Qin legal tradition; it added a public doctrine. Emperor Wu gave the Confucian classics a privileged place, and the academy that trained officials taught the Analects, the Odes, the Documents and the rites. The five classics became the curriculum of government.

The effect was to change the vocabulary of legitimacy. The Mandate of Heaven could be lost through misrule, omens were read as comment on policy, and officials could cite a text to criticise a decision without raising an army. The cost was narrowing: other schools lost the route to office, and a career required a fixed canon. The gap between the benevolent ideal and the administration became a permanent tension.

Paper, history, and a name that outlived the state

In 105 AD Cai Lun, who supervised the imperial workshops, presented a method for making sheets from tree bark, hemp scraps, rags and old fishing nets. Paper was cheaper than silk and lighter than bamboo, and the histories say it spread quickly; the surviving early fragments are later, but the attribution is consistent.

Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian had been completed around 91 BC, and Ban Gu's History of the Former Han, finished with his sister Ban Zhao, fixed the format of the dynastic history. Both preserved the census of 2 AD: about 12.2 million households and 59.6 million people. The Eastern Han, from 25 to 220 AD, moved the capital to Luoyang and lost power to consort families, eunuchs and great landowners. When it fell, the name did not: the people formerly called Huaxia came to call themselves Han ren, and the word remains the ordinary term for the majority population.
