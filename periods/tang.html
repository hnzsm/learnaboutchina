An Empire Built Out of Registers

A dynasty that governed by counting households and land, and what happened when the count stopped matching the ground.

Written and edited by one writer · Last reviewed November 2026 · Sources listed at the end

Tang is remembered for poetry, cosmopolitan markets and territory in Central Asia, but its government ran on a plainer instrument: the register. Households were counted, land was allotted on paper, adult males were listed for service, and soldiers were drawn from the same rolls. The dynasty was strong while those lists agreed with each other. When they stopped agreeing, the state rebuilt itself around property.

The household register

The statutes of 624 tied land to status. An adult male was entered for 100 mu, of which 20 mu passed to his heirs and 80 mu reverted at death; in return he owed 2 shi of grain, a length of cloth and 20 days of labour. The package, zu yong diao, treated allotment and obligation as two halves of one entry. A man who held land was visible, and a visible man could be taxed.

The count was the weak point. Registers were compiled in triplicate and revised locally, with the household as the unit for which the magistrate answered. That worked while population was low and land was available. By the eighth century holdings had been bought and divided, monasteries held land outside the rolls, and the allotment had become a schedule of entitlements rather than a description of fields. The register did not collapse. It became a record of what the state wished were true, and the tax fell on those who could not escape it.

The ward wall

The same habit produced Chang'an. The Sui plan kept by the Tang enclosed about 84 square kilometres, with 108 walled wards and two supervised markets on a grid of twenty-five avenues. The gates of each ward closed at dusk, and movement between blocks stopped until morning.

This is not the city of modern imagination. There was no continuous street life and no shopfronts along the avenues; commerce happened in the two markets, at set hours, under officials who checked weights. The grid was an administrative instrument: a ward could be counted, policed and provisioned, and its gate shut. Estimates of the population reach a million, fed by grain shipped from the south. The walls kept the population legible.

The frontier garrison

The early army was a militia of the same households. Men trained locally, equipped themselves and rotated to the capital for guard duty, which was possible because the register had given them land. The fubing system's virtue was cost: farmers who already owed service needed no standing budget.

Long frontier campaigns broke the rotation. Tours were extended and holdings abandoned. In 723 the court recruited 120,000 full-time guards, and by 737 the frontier armies were being filled by long-service volunteers paid in cash and cloth. A professional army is loyal to its paymaster, and after the 730s that meant the military governors. An Lushan held three commands and about 150,000 men; his revolt began in 755 and lasted until 763, and the centre never again monopolised force.

The examination hall

Tang did not invent government by examination, but it made the examination a public spectacle. The jinshi tested literary composition and policy, and it was the hardest regular degree. The numbers were small: about 6,400 awards over 289 years, roughly 22 a year, with a few thousand candidates reaching the final stage.

Most officials still entered through family privilege or clerical promotion. The examination mattered beyond its output because it changed family strategy. A household that could afford tutors, books and a decade of study might place one son in the capital, and his network could carry the rest. Graduates carved their names at the Wild Goose Pagoda and were feasted at Qujiang, ceremonies staged to make office look earned.

The two-tax ledger

The rebellion destroyed the fiscal base. Registered households fell from about nine million in 754 to under three million in 760, partly through death and flight, partly because people stopped appearing in a count that only brought demands.

In 780 the chancellor Yang Yan replaced the old system with two taxes, levied in summer and autumn. Assessment followed the property a household actually held and the place where it lived, not the status of its head or its ancestral registration, and part of the payment was in coin. The change accepted facts the register had denied, giving tenants and migrants a place in the assessment. But a tax stated in cash exposed payers to the value of money, and surcharges multiplied. The ledger was modernised; the politics were not.

The monastery gate

Monasteries sat outside the register precisely. Ordained monks and nuns were exempt from labour service and household tax, monastery land was often exempt from land tax, and donations removed property from the assessable pool. By the eighth century the larger houses ran mills, pawnshops and lending operations.

The state protected them because they prayed for the dynasty and supplied welfare that no ministry provided. Printing served the same purpose: the Diamond Sutra dated 868, the earliest dated printed book known, was commissioned for merit. In 845 the calculation changed. Emperor Wuzong suppressed Buddhism, and the histories record 4,600 monasteries and 40,000 shrines destroyed, 260,500 monks and nuns returned to lay life, and their land taken. The religion survived; the corporate autonomy did not.

The western market

Chang'an's foreign population is documented in objects. The western market contained lodgings for Persian merchants, Sogdian traders appear in property records as residents of named wards, and Zoroastrian shrines stood in five wards, ordinary geography rather than palace curiosities.

The sharpest evidence is epigraphy. The epitaph of a Sogdian woman named Ma, wife of Su Liang, carries Middle Persian above Chinese, and the tombs of An Jia and Shi Jun combine Chinese burial form with Central Asian imagery. A Japanese monk, Ennin, lived in the city in the 840s and described its markets and monasteries. The state administered the mixture through an office called the sabao, which supervised foreign religious affairs. Cosmopolitanism was a set of posts and permissions, and it could be revoked.

Chinatown

Tang lasted 289 years, and for the second half it was a weakened state with provincial armies, court factions and a capital taken repeatedly. Its reputation does not match that record. The poetry of the eighth and ninth centuries became the canon by which Chinese was later taught, and the canon carried the name.

The name travelled further than the government. Tang legal codes were copied in Japan, Korea and Vietnam, and overseas communities later called themselves Tang people, fixing the word as an ethnic label. The reputation records what others took from the period: less the territory than the model of a state that could absorb strangers and still govern them. The registers failed. The idea of a society open enough to need them was copied.
