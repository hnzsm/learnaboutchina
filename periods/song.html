The Dynasty That Chose the Market

A state that lost its frontier and reorganised its cities, its currency and its exams.

Written and edited by one writer · Last reviewed November 2026 · Sources listed at the end

Song lost the north, paid tribute for most of its existence and was finally destroyed by invasion. That summary is accurate about battles and misleading about nearly everything else. In the eleventh century the state stopped organising cities as walled grids, began issuing paper claims on future revenue, and made examination success the qualification for elite status. What it built was a society that invested in contracts, printing and schooling rather than in cavalry.

One wall comes down

In Tang cities, residence and trade were separated by walls and by hours. In Song Kaifeng the separation failed. Encroachments on the avenues began in the 980s, were demolished by order in 1002, and returned; by the 1030s the ward system was formally abandoned. A shopkeeper who paid the commercial tax could open on any street.

The physical change was small; the social change was not. Once a house could face the street, the street became the commercial surface of the city: signboards, awnings, teahouses above shops, pedlars between them. Late Northern Song Kaifeng counted more than 6,000 commercial households and some 8,000 small traders. Tang had markets; Song had a market economy that used the whole city.

One million people inside a wall

Kaifeng's outer wall ran about 26 kilometres, and the population inside is usually estimated at around a million by the end of the eleventh century. After the court moved south in 1127, Hangzhou grew to a comparable size. Estimates for Song China reach 100 million by 1110, roughly double the registered Tang figure of 754.

A city that size could not be fed locally. Grain came by canal from the Yangtze delta, which is why the capital sat where the Bian met the Yellow River and why the state spent so much on waterworks. The wall remained, but as a defence and customs boundary rather than a container for social life. Inside it the ward administration gave way to precincts run for policing and fire control.

One paper note replaces a cart of iron

Sichuan used heavy iron coins, and Chengdu merchants began issuing notes against deposited coin in the early eleventh century. In 1023 the government created a paper money bureau, and the first official issue followed in 1024. Notes circulated in fixed series with an expiry date, and the state learned that it could print more than it redeemed.

Salt produced the other paper instrument. Merchants delivering grain to frontier garrisons received vouchers payable in salt or cash, and the vouchers were tradable; by the twelfth century the state sold salt permits directly. In 1077 revenue reached roughly 70 million strings, about two thirds of it from monopoly and commercial levies rather than the land tax. The limit was credibility: paper worked while the state redeemed it, and Southern Song issues inflated repeatedly.

Three inventions and their limits

Movable type was devised in the 1040s by a commoner named Bi Sheng, who cut clay characters, fired them and set them in an iron frame. Shen Kuo described the process around 1088. No Song movable-type book survives, and woodblock printing stayed cheaper for large editions, so the invention was real and its adoption slow.

The compass and gunpowder followed similar paths. Shen Kuo described a magnetised needle and its deviation from north, and in 1119 Zhu Yu recorded sailors using a compass under overcast skies, having steered by the stars when it was clear. The Wujing zongyao of 1044 listed three gunpowder formulas for incendiary and smoke weapons, and the first tubular firearm appears in 1259. None transformed war in Song times; each supplemented older methods.

Three hundred graduates a year

Song made the examination the main route into office and made it regular: 118 examinations in 320 years, more than 100,000 degrees of all kinds, against roughly 6,400 jinshi in the whole Tang. Northern Song averaged about 360 men a year. The palace examination became a fixed stage, scripts were copied before marking, and a three-year cycle was established in the 1060s.

The effect was a self-conscious elite defined by learning rather than birth. Printing supplied commentaries, model answers and anthologies, and families invested in books, teachers and long preparation. The group was not open as its rhetoric claimed: cost excluded most households, and supplementary degrees absorbed repeated failures. What it produced was a national culture of office that survived the dynasty's armies.

Six clauses in a reform programme

Wang Anshi became chief councillor in 1069 and argued that revenue could rise without raising the land tax, by lending, trading and commuting obligations into cash. The green sprouts law lent grain or money at sowing and collected repayment with a 20 percent charge. The service levy law replaced labour with a cash payment, and the market exchange law set up state offices to trade in competition with merchants.

The measures were ambitious and harsh. Counties were given lending targets, so officials pressed loans on households that did not want them; cash commutation hit households with labour but little money; and market exchange offices undercut the merchants they were meant to discipline. Sima Guang and Su Shi attacked the principle that the state should compete with its own people for profit. The reforms were reversed and reinstated as factions changed, and the argument outlasted the dynasty.

Two hundred years of tribute

In 1005 the Song court agreed with the Liao at Chanzhou to pay 100,000 taels of silver and 200,000 bolts of silk a year and to fix the border. The figure rose later, and the 1141 settlement with the Jin set 250,000 of each. The sums were small against a revenue of tens of millions, which is why some historians call the treaty cheap.

Cheap in cash, expensive in structure. The dynasty had lost the sixteen prefectures, including the horse country of the north, in 938, before it existed, and never recovered a supply of cavalry mounts. Its army was recruited rather than conscripted, reaching about 1.25 million men under Emperor Renzong, and units were rotated so that no commander could build a following. Recruitment also absorbed unemployed men who might have become bandits, which made demobilisation impossible. The state could defend a line but not pursue a victory.

A thousand years of institutions

What Song left was a set of procedures. The examination remained the route to office until 1905, with the same syllabus and anonymity devices. Paper money, salt permits and monopoly licences became standard instruments of public finance into the twentieth century. The street city, shops below and dwellings above, became the normal form of the Chinese town.

Its intellectual exports were as durable. Neo-Confucian commentary, codified by Zhu Xi in the twelfth century, became the orthodoxy of later empires, and printing, porcelain and the compass travelled with the trade that paid for them. The reputation for weakness comes from measuring a state by its battles. By that measure Song failed. By the measure of what persisted, it was the most influential dynasty after the Qin. It chose the ledger, the school and the press, and those outlasted every army it raised.
