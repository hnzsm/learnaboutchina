Tradition That Still Lives

Culture in China is not locked away in a museum — it's taught in schools, practiced in teahouses, worn on the street, and celebrated every year.

Technology & Digital Life

Where the Future Is Already Normal

China's technology is not a forecast — it is the daily routine for over a billion people. QR codes instead of wallets, driverless taxis, rockets over the desert.

TECH

Hangzhou — Where Cash Is Optional

Hangzhou is the home of Alipay and the closest thing China has to a cashless city: you can buy a steamed bun, rent a bike or pay a street musician with a QR code. The same city hosts Alibaba Cloud, one of the largest cloud networks in the world.

TECH

Shenzhen — Drones, Then Everything Else

DJI, founded in a Shenzhen dorm room, now makes roughly seven of every ten consumer drones sold on Earth. Tencent grew up in the same city: its WeChat app is used by about 1.3 billion people and quietly replaced the wallet, the phone book and the notice board.

TECH

Hefei — Memory Chips and Electric Cars

Hefei turned itself into a chip city: ChangXin Memory Technologies here builds China's own DRAM memory. Electric-car maker NIO assembles its vehicles nearby, one reason Hefei is now called a new-energy capital.

TECH

Shanghai — The Livestream Capital

In Shanghai, shopping is a live show. Hosts broadcast for hours from studios stacked with cosmetics and parcels, answering comments between sales. A single good night can move more goods than a department store sells in a month.

TECH

Beijing — 45,000 km of High-Speed Rail

China's high-speed rail network is the largest on Earth, long enough to wrap around the planet. From Beijing's vast stations, Fuxing trains glide at 350 km/h, and the 12306 app sells close to a billion tickets a year.

TECH

Wuhan — Robotaxis on the Street

Wuhan is one of the world's biggest testing grounds for driverless taxis: hail a car through an app and it arrives with nobody in the driver's seat. Locals treat it as an everyday curiosity rather than a science-fiction scene.

TECH

Jiuquan — Rockets Over the Gobi

From a launch centre in the Gobi Desert, China sends Shenzhou crews and Tiangong station modules into orbit. Launches have become routine enough that people living near Jiuquan simply check the sky at dinner time.

Chinese Lifestyle & Wellness

How a Billion People Actually Live

Between the big headlines is a quieter set of habits: parks at dawn, teahouses at noon, and a thermos of hot water wherever you go.

LIFESTYLE

Chengdu — Teahouses and Mahjong

Chengdu's teahouses are the city's living room: bamboo chairs, covered porcelain cups, a thermos on the table, and a mahjong game that can run from breakfast to dinner. Nobody is in a hurry, and that is the whole point.

LIFESTYLE

Square Dancing at Dusk

Every evening, from village squares to city plazas, speakers come out and groups dance in unison. Square dancing is free, social and open to any age — one of the most visible and least reported habits of modern China.

LIFESTYLE

Tai Chi Before Breakfast

Walk into almost any park at six in the morning and you will find people moving through slow, precise taijiquan forms under the trees. It is exercise, meditation and a neighbourhood meeting all at once.

LIFESTYLE

Guangzhou — Morning Tea, Cantonese Style

In Guangzhou, morning tea is a full breakfast and a social institution. Bamboo baskets of dumplings arrive one after another, the teapot is refilled without being asked, and nobody leaves before the last plate is empty.

LIFESTYLE

Hangzhou — The Art of a Perfect Pour

The gongfu tea ceremony turns a simple cup into a slow ritual: rinse the leaves, warm the pot, then pour three or four infusions, each with its own flavour. Hangzhou's Longjing green tea is the most famous in the country.

LIFESTYLE

Yangzhou — The Art of Bathing

Yangzhou is famous for its bathhouses, where an afternoon passes between hot pools, tea and a scrub-and-massage that regulars swear by. The city calls it the art of water and slow time.

LIFESTYLE

Dengfeng — Kung Fu at the Source

The Shaolin Temple in Henan is the birthplace of Chan Buddhism and of martial arts as most of the world knows them. Its warrior monks still train every day, performing staff and fist forms for visitors.

LIFESTYLE

Bozhou — Herbs, Cupping and Slow Cures

Traditional Chinese medicine is everyday practice rather than folklore: cupping, moxibustion and acupuncture are widely used, and plenty of office workers carry a thermos of goji and red dates. Bozhou runs the country's largest herb market.

Traditional Aesthetics & Craft

Made by Hand, for 3,000 Years

Paper, porcelain, silk and ink. Chinese craft traditions were industrial before industry existed, and many of the old workshops are still working.

CRAFT

Xi'an — Writing as the Highest Art

Calligraphy is treated as the highest of the Chinese arts, a discipline in which a single stroke can reveal rhythm, balance and calm. The toolkit is the Four Treasures: brush, ink stick, paper and ink stone.

CRAFT

Suzhou — Silk, Needles and Hanfu

Suzhou embroidery can be finished on both sides of the same piece of silk, so finely that a small work takes months. The same city is today the capital of the hanfu revival, where young people rent and buy traditional dress.

CRAFT

Beijing — Opera Behind the Paint

Peking Opera packs singing, acrobatics and dance into a single evening. Its painted faces work as a code — red for loyalty, black for boldness, white for cunning — and the four roles, sheng, dan, jing and chou, each move differently.

CRAFT

Jingdezhen — The Porcelain Capital

Jingdezhen has fired porcelain for more than a thousand years and gave the world the blue-and-white decoration everyone recognises. Craftsmen still paint the cobalt patterns by hand, one brushstroke at a time.

CRAFT

Chengdu — Brocade and a Loom of Gold

Shu brocade is woven from silk so fine it was once used to pay imperial officials. The pattern is built thread by thread on a wooden loom, and the technique has barely changed in two thousand years.

CRAFT

Jingxian — Paper, Ink and a Hundred Steps

Xuan paper from Anhui is made from tree bark and rice straw in a process of roughly a hundred steps, and it is prized for surviving centuries of brushwork. With it come Hui ink sticks and She ink stones — the complete scholar's kit.

Film, Games & Web Fiction

China's New Storytellers

A generation that grew up on games, web novels and ninety-second dramas is now exporting its stories — along with the production lines behind them.

SCREEN

Black Myth: Wukong

A Chinese studio's reworking of the Monkey King legend became one of the best-selling games in the world and sent players to the real temples and mountain landscapes that were scanned into it.

SCREEN

Hengdian — China's Hollywood

Hengdian is the largest film studio complex on Earth: a full-scale copy of the Forbidden City, streets from every dynasty, and crews at work around the clock. Most of China's costume dramas are shot here.

SCREEN

The Three-Body Problem

Liu Cixin's novel turned Chinese science fiction into a global conversation. It won the Hugo Award and has been adapted for television on both sides of the Pacific, and its scale is the point: civilisations, physics and the cold dark between them.

SCREEN

Mini-Dramas, 90 Seconds at a Time

Short vertical dramas — shot quickly, watched on a phone, often one complete story per evening — have become a real industry, with hundreds of millions of viewers and crews filming in apartment blocks across the country.

SCREEN

Shanghai — The Esports Capital

Shanghai has become the centre of Chinese esports, hosting world finals in arenas that sell out in minutes. Leagues run all year, and playing games professionally now comes with coaching staffs and training camps.

SCREEN

Beijing — The Web-Fiction Empire

Serialised web novels are read by hundreds of millions of people on their phones, updated daily by writers who live on subscriptions and reader tips. Entire genres grew out of the comment section.

SCREEN

Chengdu — Ink, Pixels and Ne Zha

Chengdu's animation studios mixed classical Chinese painting with modern 3D and produced one of the highest-grossing animated films ever made. The city now trains a whole generation of animators.

Festivals & Folk Customs

When the Whole Country Celebrates

Chinese festivals are collective to a degree that is hard to describe: hundreds of millions of people moving, cooking and lighting lanterns at the same time.

FESTIVALS

Spring Festival Temple Fairs

Lunar New Year sets off the largest annual migration on Earth, and temple fairs are the party at the end of the journey: red lanterns, lion dances, snack stalls and fortune-tellers for the first fifteen days of the year.

FESTIVALS

Harbin — A City Carved From Ice

Every winter Harbin builds a city out of river ice, complete with palaces, slides and towers lit from the inside. It is rebuilt and melted down again every single year.

FESTIVALS

Dragon Boat Racing

Dragon boats race on lakes and rivers every summer, in memory of an ancient poet. Crews train all year for a race that is over in minutes, and the drumbeat sets the whole rhythm.

FESTIVALS

Nanjing — The Qinhuai Lantern Fair

Along Nanjing's Qinhuai river, the Lantern Festival fills the water with thousands of red and gold lanterns for weeks, a tradition recorded since the fifth century.

FESTIVALS

Xishuangbanna — The Water Splashing Festival

For the Dai people of Yunnan, the New Year is celebrated by splashing water on everyone in sight: the more water, the more good fortune. Nobody stays dry, and nobody minds.

FESTIVALS

Liangshan — The Torch Festival

Yi communities light torches, dance around bonfires and stay up all night in a festival celebrating fire, harvest and community. Mountainside villages glow for miles.

FESTIVALS

Guangzhou — The New Year Flower Market

In Guangzhou the Lunar New Year begins in the flower market, where the whole city buys peach branches, kumquat trees and orchids. The Cantonese word for kumquat also means prosperity.

FESTIVALS

Fujian — Youshen, Gods on Parade

In Fujian, village deities are carried out of their temples on carved palanquins and paraded through the streets with drums and fireworks. Youshen is loud, crowded and deeply local — and it has become a viral spectacle.

Safety & City Governance

Millions of People, One Control Room

Chinese cities run at a scale that forces new tools: data dashboards, one-stop hotlines, and utility tunnels built before the first resident moves in.

GOVERNANCE

Hangzhou — City Brain

Hangzhou's traffic system reads cameras and sensors across the city and retunes traffic lights in real time. City Brain began as a way to clear ambulances through congestion and now serves dozens of cities.

GOVERNANCE

Beijing — One Number for Everything

Beijing routes residents' complaints about potholes, rubbish, noise or heating into a single hotline, 12345, and measures how quickly each one is closed. Officials are judged on the response rate.

GOVERNANCE

Shanghai — One Net, One Grid

Shanghai divides a city of 25 million people into small grids, each with its own duty officer and data feed, so problems are reported and solved locally. It is an unglamorous but effective way to run a megacity.

GOVERNANCE

Shenzhen — Government in an App

In Shenzhen most paperwork happens on a phone: residency, permits, appointments and payments through a single city app. The service halls are bright and new, but the queue is mostly digital.

GOVERNANCE

Xiong'an — A City Built Smart

Xiong'an is being built from an empty landscape with its utilities already underground: power, water and even freight tunnels were laid before the first offices opened. It is a test case for how Chinese cities may be planned next.

GOVERNANCE

Chongqing — Taming a Mountain City

Chongqing stacks roads, rails and buildings onto hillsides, so traffic has to be managed in three dimensions: elevated monorails run straight through apartment blocks, and bridges climb between cliffs.

By the Numbers

Culture Is a Scale Thing

8,000+

Spoken dialects recognized in China, though Mandarin is the official language. Many are mutually unintelligible — think of Cantonese vs. Sichuanese as different languages with a shared written script.

56

Ethnic groups officially recognized. The Han are 92% of the population, but Uyghurs, Tibetans, Mongols, Zhuang, Miao, and dozens more each have their own language, clothing, and traditions.

55

World Heritage Sites. The Great Wall, the Forbidden City, West Lake, Leshan Giant Buddha, Mogao Caves, and more — China has more UNESCO sites than Italy and France combined.
