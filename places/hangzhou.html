Three Sides Mountain, One Side City

A lagoon on the edge of a delta, kept shallow on purpose, which became the capital of half an empire.

Written and edited by one writer · Last reviewed November 2026 · Sources listed at the end

West Lake is not a natural lake in the sense that most visitors assume. It is a lagoon on the edge of the Qiantang estuary that would have silted up centuries ago if people had left it alone. The city beside it, and the imperial court that moved into it in 1138, are both downstream of a single fact: somebody had to keep dredging.

The site, in one sentence

The phrase locals use is "three sides of cloud-wrapped hills, one side of city". Mountains ring the lake on the south, west and north, and the urban plain runs east toward the river. That shape explains almost everything: it made the lake a basin that collected silt, it made the hills a source of springs and tea terraces, and it made the city's growth one-directional.

The hills also gave the lake its pictures. For about a thousand years, painters and poets have organised views of West Lake around the same components — a bridge, a low island, a pagoda on a ridge, willows at the water's edge. The "ten scenes" of West Lake were named in the Southern Song period, and they are essentially a list of compositions that later artists repeated.

What a capital does to a lagoon

In 1138 the Song court, pushed south by the Jurchen Jin, settled at Lin'an, the city now called Hangzhou, and made it the capital of the southern half of the empire. The population grew fast, and a capital needs water: for drinking, for fire-fighting, for canals, and for the boats that carried grain and fuel.

A shallow lake in a populated basin silts. Sediment arrives from the streams, algae blooms in the warm water, and the lake slowly becomes marsh. The remedy has always been dredging, and dredging has always been a political act, because it costs money, employs thousands, and produces spoil that has to go somewhere. Records of lake works go back centuries and include projects ordered by successive prefects, including the poet Bai Juyi in the 820s.

The causeway that is, and the causeway that is not

In 1090, Su Shi — poet, official, and prefect of the city — organised a major dredging. The spoil was used to build an embankment across the lake, about 2.8 kilometres long, with six bridges, now called the Su Causeway. It is one of the few public works in China with a poet's name on it, and it still functions: it divides the lake, carries a walkway, and creates the long views that every photograph uses.

The other famous causeway, the Bai Causeway, is named for Bai Juyi, who also dredged the lake. But the embankment that carries his name today is not the one he built. The present causeway dates from later centuries and was named in his memory — a useful reminder that a place's most beloved stories are often the ones with the loosest chronology.

Both causeways are the same idea in different centuries: the maintenance of the lake generates the landscape. The thing that looks most natural about West Lake is the by-product of engineering.

The canal at the other end of the city

Hangzhou is the southern terminus of the Grand Canal, the waterway that runs roughly 1,800 kilometres north to Beijing. The canal was built and rebuilt across several dynasties, and its economic function was to move grain from the fertile south to the capital. Hangzhou's position at the terminus made it a collection point, a warehouse city, and a place where goods changed hands before moving north.

That is why the city has two water systems with different characters. The lake is scenic and managed for views; the canal is commercial and managed for tonnage. The canal still runs through the urban fabric, crossed by arched stone bridges, and its warehouses and wharves shaped the streets around it.

Tea in the hills behind the water

The hills west of the lake grow Longjing tea, and the village of Longjing sits among the terraces. The tea is pan-fired by hand in a process that flattens the leaves and produces the characteristic chestnut aroma. The most famous bushes in the village are the eighteen imperial bushes, supposedly singled out by the Qianlong emperor on a visit in the eighteenth century — a story that is better treated as legend than as record, but which tells you exactly how tea is valued here: by provenance and by story.

Tea also explains the hills. Terraces, paths, and the small settlements that serviced them are part of the same landscape that UNESCO inscribed in 2011 as a cultural landscape, recognising that West Lake is not a scenic spot with a city attached but a piece of territory shaped by centuries of use.

What to notice, rather than what to see

The lake is about fifteen kilometres around, shallow, and almost entirely ringed by a road and a path. The interesting thing is how many distinct views have been composed within that ring: a bridge seen from a pavilion, a pagoda seen through willows, an island seen across water. Each was designed to be looked at from a particular point, and each depends on the lake being shallow enough for reflections and still enough for mist.

Leifeng Pagoda, on the southern hill, collapsed in 1924 and was rebuilt in 2002; the ruins are preserved beneath the new structure. The rebuilding is a fair summary of how the city treats its heritage — the view matters, the object is replaceable, and the two are not the same thing.

Modern Hangzhou is also a technology city, and the lake is still the centre of gravity. The office towers are east, the tea hills are west, and the water in the middle is the reason the city has a shape at all.
