A Capital That Kept Being Declared, and Kept Being Left

Six dynasties, one Ming founder, a rebel kingdom and a republic all ruled from here. None of them stayed, and the wall they left still sets the city's height.

Written and edited by one writer · Last reviewed November 2026 · Sources listed at the end

Nanjing has been declared a capital by six dynasties, by the first Ming emperor, by the Taiping and by the Republic of China. It lost the position every time, usually within a lifetime. That cycle, rather than any single reign, explains the city's shape: a Ming wall that still bounds the centre, a street pattern partly inherited from a sixth-century grid, and a skyline kept low by rules. The question is not why rulers came. It is what they left when they went.

229: A capital that kept coming back

Sun Quan of Wu made Jianye his capital in 229, and for most of the next three and a half centuries the same site held the seat of a southern state. Wu was followed by the Eastern Jin and by the four southern dynasties, Song, Qi, Liang and Chen. The city counts its six dynasties from that sequence.

The site earned the repetition: hills to the east, the Yangtze to the north-west, a delta that produced grain and rivers that moved it. The rhythm mattered more. Each northern conquest demoted the city to a provincial seat; each new southern regime promoted it again and built on the ground the last one had used.

317: The court arrives, and the city becomes an argument

In 317 the Jin court, having lost the north, re-established itself at Jiankang. The move made the city the capital of a claim as much as of a territory: court ritual, aristocratic families and the northern literary language were carried south and installed here.

Almost none of it survives above ground. The palace city, Taicheng, was about eight li around, and its core lies beneath the Daxinggong area. Archaeologists place the outer city between Zhujiang Road, Longpan Middle Road, Xinjiekou and Baixia Road. A stretch of palace axis survives in the Nanjing Library, with wall remains in the Six Dynasties Museum.

The Six Dynasties city is therefore not a ruin to walk around but a buried plan the living city partly follows, with some streets still near the old axes. Xuanwu Lake, used by the southern courts for naval practice, still lies inside the later Ming wall.

1366: A wall that recorded who made it

Zhu Yuanzhang began the wall in 1366, two years before he proclaimed the Ming, and the work was finished in the early 1390s. It ran 35.267 kilometres, of which 25.091 kilometres still stand, the longest surviving city wall of brick and stone anywhere. It is not a rectangle: the line climbs hills, takes in lakes and follows the Qinhuai, because the Ming built for the terrain.

The brick is the document. Some 350 million bricks were fired across the lower Yangzi provinces, and most carry stamped inscriptions naming the county, the supervising official, the kiln and the mason. The practice was called wu le gong ming, marking the artisan's name on the object: a failed batch could be traced to the people who made it.

Zhonghua Gate, the former Jubao Gate, shows the wall's purpose: three inner barbicans, four arched gateways in sequence, and 27 chambers built into the ramparts. A gate that size is a machine for making an attacker spend time where he can be shot at.

1421: The government leaves, the scale stays

In 1421 the Yongle emperor moved the court to Beijing. Nanjing kept the title of southern capital and a reduced set of ministries, but the political weight went north. The palace built here between 1366 and 1392 had already served as the model for Beijing: gate, court and hall in the same axial sequence.

What remained was the container: broad thoroughfares, oversized gates and a walled area far larger than the population needed. For five centuries Nanjing was the seat of a province and a viceroy, living inside a shell built for an empire. The Ming palace is now fragmentary, gate bases and place names, and the city thinned out around its imperial centre rather than building over it.

1899: The riverbank turns outward

The Treaty of Nanjing was signed in 1842 on a British warship off Xiaguan, ending the First Opium War. It did not open Nanjing: the five ports were Guangzhou, Xiamen, Fuzhou, Ningbo and Shanghai. Nanjing was opened under the settlements of 1858, and formally in May 1899, when Xiaguan became a trading port with a district outside Yifeng Gate.

That created a second city with a different orientation. The walled city faced inward, around its offices and examination hall. Xiaguan faced the Yangtze, with wharves, warehouses, a customs house, and later the railway and the Pukou ferry. For half a century Nanjing had two edges, administrative and commercial, with open ground between them.

1927: Capital again, this time on paper

In 1927 the Nationalist government made Nanjing capital of the Republic, and in 1929 it published the Capital Plan, prepared with the American architect Henry Murphy as adviser: zoning, a road grid, a new administrative district, and a style mixing modern construction with Chinese roofs.

Most of it was never built, but what was built rearranged the city. Zhongshan Road was driven through the old fabric, and the old Chaoyang Gate was demolished and rebuilt with three arches for the funeral procession of Sun Yat-sen in 1929. The boulevards, ministries and universities stayed. The government left in 1937 and again in 1949.

1937: What the city carries

Japanese forces entered Nanjing on 13 December 1937. In the weeks that followed, prisoners of war and civilians were killed in large numbers across the city and its suburbs, in atrocities documented at the time by foreign residents and afterwards by the International Military Tribunal for the Far East and the Nanjing War Crimes Tribunal.

Estimates of the dead differ among historians. The figure of 300,000 is the one recorded by the Chinese tribunals and carried by the memorial at Jiangdongmen, which opened in 1985 on the site of a mass grave. A Safety Zone organised by foreign residents sheltered refugees in the centre while the killing continued outside it.

This is not a separate chapter of the urban history. The wall was the line of defence, the gates were the points of entry, and the geography of the massacre is the geography of the old city.

2020s: A skyline kept low on purpose

Central Nanjing does not look like a provincial capital of its size. Inside the Ming wall there are few tall buildings, and the reason is written in planning rules: about 35 metres, or 40 for public buildings, in the three historic areas; 18 metres along the wall, around Xuanwu Lake, along Yudao Street and in the southern historic city; 12 metres south of Jiqing and Changle roads, with eaves capped at 7 metres near the wall.

Growth went elsewhere, chiefly to Hexi New Town across the river, so the old city kept a low silhouette of wall, lake, hill and residential block. That has costs: a centre that empties at night, commutes across the river, a protected fabric expensive to maintain. The low skyline is not the absence of a decision. It is the decision.
