The City That Grew Upward Instead of Outward

An oasis at the western edge of the Tarim Basin, where exchange and a shortage of flat ground shaped a vertical town.

Written and edited by one writer · Last reviewed November 2026 · Sources listed at the end

Kashgar sits where the mountains close in at the western end of the Tarim Basin. The old city reflects that position in its plan: a place where caravans regrouped before the passes, where land on the terrace ran out before a family's sons did, and where a house could grow by adding a room above the room next door. The result is dense, vertical and still inhabited.

At the western end of the route

The Tarim Basin is a bowl of desert ringed by mountains. At its western edge the Pamir, the Tian Shan and the Kunlun ranges converge, and the Kashgar river carries meltwater from the Pamirs into an oasis. Routes along the northern and southern rims of the basin meet near this point before climbing west over the Pamirs, north over the Tian Shan, or south toward the Karakoram. Kashgar was therefore a junction and a staging ground, not a terminus.

A caravan arriving from the east needed water, fodder, animals and a market before the passes; a caravan coming down from the mountains needed the same things in reverse. That double demand made the oasis a place where goods changed hands, and where information, credit and languages travelled with them. The city's importance came from the gap between the desert and the mountains, not from anything produced inside its walls.

Above the river, a city that grew upward

The historic core lies on the north bank of the Kashgar river, partly on a loess terrace above the flood plain. In the Qiasa and Yawagh quarters the lanes are narrow and the houses press against one another. Land on the terrace was finite, and inheritance divided it further, so the practical direction of growth was vertical: a room added above a room, an upper floor reached by an outside stair, a passage carried over the lane as a bridge.

The construction follows the logic of the materials. Walls are adobe or rammed earth, floors and roofs rest on poplar beams, and shaded verandas and courtyards moderate the summer heat. The high terrace quarter, Gaotai, is the extreme case: houses stacked two to four storeys, some higher, on a cliff above the river, with more than forty lanes threading between them. The old city is not a uniform fabric. It is a set of solutions to the same shortage of flat ground.

At the Id Kah, the city keeps time

The Id Kah Mosque stands on the central square, founded in 1442 and enlarged over the following centuries. It is the largest mosque in China, covering about 16,800 square metres, with a yellow gate, a long courtyard and a prayer hall whose inner and outer sections are carried on 140 carved wooden columns. The name is usually explained as a place of festival, and the square in front has long served as the city's common room.

The mosque sets a weekly and annual rhythm rather than a scenic one. Friday prayer draws a larger crowd; the two Eid festivals fill the square and the surrounding streets, and the movement of people between home, mosque and bazaar is part of how the old city works. For a visitor the building is an architectural monument; for the city it is a timetable.

On Sunday, the livestock market

The livestock market, known locally as Mal Bazar, is held on Sundays on level ground outside the built-up area. Herders bring sheep, goats, cattle, horses and occasionally camels. Buyers inspect teeth, lift animals, feel the back and agree a sale through a broker, and most of the trading is finished by midday.

The market is a working institution, not a performance of one. It connects the pastoral economy of the surrounding mountains with the meat and dairy demand of the oasis, and it does so through credit, reputation and a broker's memory rather than a written contract. The handshake is not a tourist custom; it is how a deal is closed when both parties expect to trade again.

In the tomb, a family and a story

The Apak Khoja complex outside the old city began in the early seventeenth century as a religious school and a family mausoleum. The tomb chamber was built for Muhammad Yusuf Khoja in about 1640, and later generations of the Khoja family were buried in the same complex. Its green-tiled dome, low and wide, is a landmark of Uyghur Islamic architecture in Xinjiang.

Chinese popular accounts call the site the tomb of the Fragrant Concubine, Xiangfei, a Uyghur consort associated with the Qianlong emperor. The label is a later story. The historical consort, Imperial Noble Consort Rong, died in 1788 and was buried at the Eastern Qing tombs near Beijing; the Kashgar connection was attached to the site in the late nineteenth century and has been repeated ever since. The mausoleum can be understood as a family tomb that acquired a national legend.

After 1890, the consulates

In the late nineteenth century the rivalry between the Russian and British empires turned Kashgar into a listening post. Russia opened a consulate in 1882; Britain's representative, George Macartney, arrived in 1890, and the British consulate was formally established in 1908. The diplomats collected maps, trade figures and rumours, and the city acquired a small European quarter and a new set of buildings.

The consulates did not industrialise the oasis or turn it into a treaty port on the coast. Their effect was narrower: they made Kashgar a place where information about roads, passes and markets was gathered and transmitted, and they tied the city into imperial rivalries that had little to do with its own trade. The buildings remain, but the episode can be read as a layer of diplomacy over an older commercial town.

Now, a city still lived in

In 2010 the regional and municipal authorities began a large programme of structural reinforcement and rehousing in the old city, described in official accounts as repair in the old style, house by house. The programme addressed real problems: many buildings were unsafe, overcrowded and without modern services. Official figures record 49,083 households and 5.07 million square metres of dilapidated housing, with more than 7,000 historic dwellings repaired rather than replaced. Independent reporting at the time described extensive demolition, and the gap between the two accounts is itself part of the site's history.

The tension is not whether the old city should change, but who gets to decide how. A preserved shell with no residents becomes a stage set; a district repaired only for visitors loses the workshops, schools and households that made it a city. Kashgar's old city is unusual because it is still a place where people live, pray, trade and argue, and that is also what makes its conservation difficult.

The city's future will be decided less by its monuments than by the ordinary economy of the lanes: whether families can afford to remain, whether repair grants reach the houses that need them, and whether the bazaar remains a market rather than a backdrop. The architecture is remarkable, but the mechanism that produced it was a working exchange, and that is the part hardest to preserve.
