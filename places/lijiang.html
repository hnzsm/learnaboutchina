A Town That Follows Water

No grid, no fortress wall, and a street plan that bends because the canal bends. Lijiang was built around a trade route, and the shape of the town still records it.

Written and edited by one writer · Last reviewed November 2026 · Sources listed at the end

Lijiang is often described as a preserved old town, which suggests it was planned and then frozen. Neither is quite true. It grew as a service town on a mountain trade route, its layout was dictated by water rather than geometry, and the version you walk through today is partly a reconstruction after a 1996 earthquake. That history is more interesting than the postcard, because it explains why the streets behave the way they do.

A market on a road, not a capital

The town sits at about 2,400 m in northwestern Yunnan, at the foot of a mountain range and on the edge of the Tibetan plateau. That position put it on the Tea Horse Road, the caravan route that moved tea and salt from Yunnan into Tibet and brought horses, hides and wool back. Lijiang was where mules were fed, loads were re-packed, and deals were made.

Trade towns grow differently from capitals. Nobody imposed a rectangle. Shops clustered along the route, courtyards filled the land behind them, and lanes formed where people actually walked. The result looks organic because it is: the town is a record of traffic, not a plan.

Water came before the streets

Springs at the base of the mountains feed a pool at the northern edge of the old town, and the water was divided into three channels that run through the settlement. Every main lane has a channel beside it; every few dozen metres there is a stone slab or timber bridge. The town is not built around a central square, it is built around a water system.

That system was managed, not accidental. There were rules about who could divert water and when, and the flow was used for drinking, washing, irrigation and, traditionally, for washing the streets at night when the traffic stopped. If you wonder why the old town feels cool and shaded, the channels are part of the answer.

The Mu family and the palace on the hill

For centuries Lijiang was governed by a local Naxi family, the Mu, who held authority under the Yuan, Ming and Qing dynasties. Their residence sat on the higher ground at the western edge of the old town, above the lanes, and it was the one part of Lijiang laid out like an official building rather than a merchant street.

The family's rule lasted around 470 years across 22 generations — a long run by the standards of Chinese local elites, and a reminder that frontier regions were often governed through negotiated local power rather than direct appointments from the capital. The palace standing today is largely a 1990s reconstruction; the site and the layout are historical, the timber is not.

Dongba: writing that is still a picture

The Naxi have a ritual script, Dongba, that is pictographic rather than phonetic: signs stand for things and ideas, not for sounds. Around 1,400 distinct characters are usually counted, and they were used by Dongba priests to record myths, rituals, medicine and genealogies. It is not a script for everyday writing; it is a script for remembering what must not be forgotten.

UNESCO listed the Dongba manuscripts in its Memory of the World register in 2003. What makes them worth knowing about is the mismatch between the script and the language: a reader has to know the story to read the page, because the signs are prompts rather than a full transcription. It is closer to a musical score than to an alphabet.

The mountain that pays for the town

Jade Dragon Snow Mountain rises to about 5,596 m north of the town, with thirteen main peaks and a glacier that survives at this latitude because of altitude. It is the reason the valley has water, the reason the light in the old town is so sharp, and the reason tourism replaced trade as the local economy.

The Naxi treated the mountain as sacred, and the older villages on its lower slopes — Baisha, with its Ming-dynasty murals, and Shuhe, a smaller stop on the caravan road — are worth more time than the cable car station. Baisha in particular shows the same layering as Lijiang itself: a small settlement that was important before the bigger town beside it was.

What the 1996 earthquake changed

On 3 February 1996 a magnitude 7.0 earthquake struck the region. Several hundred people died, and a large share of the old town's buildings were damaged or destroyed. Reconstruction followed quickly, and Lijiang was inscribed as a World Heritage site the following year, in 1997.

That sequence matters when you look at the town. Some of the timber is original, much is replaced, and the commercial frontage along the main channels is newer than it appears. This is not a reason to skip Lijiang; it is a reason to look past the shopfronts at the structure — the channels, the bridges, the courtyards set back from the street, the way the ground rises toward the Mu residence.

The gorge north of town

About an hour's drive north, the Yangtze's upper reaches are squeezed between two massifs at Tiger Leaping Gorge, where the river runs roughly 3,900 m below the ridge line. The name comes from a legend about a hunter's quarry escaping across the narrowest point. The upper trail is a genuine mountain path with exposure and loose rock, not a boardwalk, and the villages along it are still farmed.

It is the clearest demonstration of what this corner of Yunnan is: vertical country, where the difference between the river and the ridge is measured in kilometres rather than metres, and where every route is a negotiation with the slope.
