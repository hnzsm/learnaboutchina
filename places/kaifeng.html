The City That Survived by Being Buried

Six walled towns, one street plan, and a river that raised the ground every time it destroyed the place.

Written and edited by one writer · Last reviewed November 2026 · Sources listed at the end

Kaifeng is the rare major Chinese city whose plan is easiest to read as a vertical section. Six walled towns, from a Warring States capital to the Qing city, lie between three and twelve metres below the present streets, separated by bands of Yellow River silt. That fact is usually told as a curiosity, and it deserves better. The river that buried Kaifeng is the river that made it possible, and the transport geography that made it a capital is the geography that later demoted it.

Why does the city sit on top of itself?

The Yellow River runs a few kilometres north of the old town on a bed higher than the streets, and levees hold it there. When a levee fails, water pours into the walled area, stops moving and drops its load of silt, which has nowhere to drain: the wall that kept the flood out also kept it in. Each serious flood raises the ground inside the walls by a metre or more.

The city has been flooded repeatedly, and the usual count is seven times. The worst came in 1642, during a siege, when dikes were breached. Modern estimates put the dead in the hundreds of thousands out of a population near 378,000, and the city was largely abandoned until reconstruction began in 1662. Rebuilding meant levelling the debris and building on the new surface, because the old surface was underneath it.

What survives when a river buries a capital?

Silt is destructive and preservative at once. It crushes roofs and buries streets, but below the water table it seals deposits from air, and waterlogged ground preserves wood, seeds, lacquer and bone. The archaeology of Kaifeng is therefore rich in ordinary things: bridge timbers, grain in a granary, the ruts of carts.

The clearest section comes from the Zhouqiao site, where excavation exposed a Song riverbank and bridge abutment, a Ming bridge above it, and a Qing road with wheel ruts above that. The wall, 14.4 kilometres around and about eight metres high, stands on the line of its predecessors and is itself Ming work repaired in the Qing. Because the axis was reused continuously, the street plan is older than every building on it.

Why is the Iron Pagoda made of glazed brick?

The pagoda of Youguo Temple was completed in 1049, five years after lightning destroyed its wooden predecessor. The replacement was fired brick faced in glazed tiles: thirteen storeys, octagonal, 55.88 metres tall, with more than fifty moulded motifs on the surface. The colour reads as iron at a distance, which is where the name comes from; the tiles themselves run through red-brown, blue and green.

The material is a piece of reasoning about this city. Timber burns, and a crowded town of timber with open hearths burns often. Fired brick does not, and a glazed skin sheds rainwater instead of soaking it, which matters where the ground is damp and floods recur. The tower also records the ground: its base is now buried, and only 55.08 metres show. The pagoda did not sink. The city rose around it.

What did it mean to knock down the ward walls?

In the Tang city the ward was the unit of order: a walled block with gates, a curfew, and markets confined to separate enclosures. From the late Tang those walls were breached by shopkeepers who wanted street frontage, and the state first resisted and then legislated around the change. By the Northern Song the ward survived as an administrative district without walls.

What followed was a city shaped by rent and traffic rather than by decree. Shops opened onto the street, houses too, and upper floors became taverns over the same narrow plot. The night market kept hours that Tang officials would have prosecuted; the memoir of the eastern capital describes trade running past the third watch and starting again before dawn.

Who put out the fires?

A dense city of timber, heated rooms and oil lamps is a city that expects to burn. The Song answer was a standing service rather than a volunteer one. Posts known as junxunpu stood roughly every three hundred paces, each with five soldiers who patrolled at night to enforce lamp curfews and raise the alarm.

Above them were watchtowers on high ground, manned day and night, with a depot of a hundred or more men below and equipment kept ready: buckets, wet sand, hooks, axes and ladders. These were soldiers. The xiang, the unit that gave the urban district its name, was a military wing before it became a civil administration.

Where did paper money come from?

Not from Kaifeng, and the correction matters. Paper currency emerged in Sichuan, where the coinage was iron, and iron is heavy: a string of it buys little and weighs a great deal. Merchants issued deposit receipts against metal in their shops. In 1023 the state took the system over by creating an office for the note, and the first official notes followed in 1024.

Kaifeng supplies the demand rather than the invention. A city of that density, with a large garrison paid in cash and a government collecting more revenue in money, needs instruments that move value without moving metal. It also introduced a new risk: a note is a promise, and a state that prints too many breaks it. The Song sequence of issue limits and inflation is one of the earliest recorded failures of monetary policy.

How does a capital lose its river?

The water system had four channels: the Bian, the Cai, the Wuzhang and the Jinshui. The Jinshui supplied the city; the others carried freight, and the Bian was decisive, running southeast toward the Huai and the Yangtze. The dynastic history records millions of shi of grain arriving in a year for the capital and its army.

A canal that carries silt must be dredged, and dredging costs more each generation as the bed rises. The Bian decayed. After 1127 the political centre moved south, then north to Beijing, and the Yuan built the Grand Canal on a new line through Shandong. Kaifeng lost the route, and flood defence finished the argument: a capital behind levees, dependent on works far upstream, is expensive to keep.

Why is Kaifeng smaller than the city it used to be?

The comparison needs care. Northern Song Kaifeng is usually estimated at between 700,000 and a million people inside and around the walls, which made it one of the largest cities on earth. The prefecture today holds just under five million, but a prefecture includes counties and farmland. Both figures are true and neither answers the question.

The demotion had three causes, arriving in order. The canal went first, when the trunk route moved to Shandong. The railway went second, when the junction went to Zhengzhou, where the north-south line of 1906 crossed the east-west one. The administration went last, in 1954, when the provincial capital moved there as well. What remains is a layered site with a tourism economy on top, restaging a Song that reads more clearly in section drawings.
