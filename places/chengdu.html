Two Thousand Years of Water, Without a Wall of Concrete

A river split in three, a plain turned into a garden, and a city that has been comfortable for a very long time.

Written and edited by one writer · Last reviewed November 2026 · Sources listed at the end

Chengdu's founding story is not a dynasty or a battle. It is a piece of hydraulic engineering on the Min River, built in 256 BC, which made a flood-prone basin into one of the most reliable agricultural regions in China. Everything else the city is known for — brocade, paper money, teahouses, a famously unhurried pace — sits on top of that solved problem.

A river with two bad habits

The Min River comes out of the mountains west of the plain with a steep gradient and a heavy sediment load. In flood season it carries gravel and silt; in the dry season it drops. A conventional dam would have ponded water behind it, filled the reservoir with stone and sand within a few decades, and created a single point of failure above a densely farmed plain.

Li Bing, the Qin-appointed governor of Shu, chose a different approach. Instead of stopping the river, he divided it, and used the river's own energy to keep the works clear. The system has no dam in the modern sense and has survived for more than two thousand years with maintenance rather than replacement.

Three pieces, none of them a barrier

The first is the Fish Mouth, a wedge-shaped levee that stands in the river and splits it into an inner channel and an outer channel. The inner channel feeds the irrigation plain; the outer channel carries flood flow and sediment away. The wedge is aligned so that the proportion of water entering each channel shifts with the river level — more to the inner channel in the dry season, more to the outer in a flood.

The second is the Flying Sand Spillway, a low overflow weir in the inner channel. When the inner channel rises too high, water spills over it back into the outer river. Because the spillway is set at a low level and the flow crosses it at an angle, it also throws sediment out of the system. That is the elegant part: the same structure that limits flooding also cleans the channel.

The third is the Bottle-Neck Channel, a narrow cut blasted through the rock of the hill on the inner side. Its width sets a hard ceiling on how much water can enter the plain, regardless of what the river is doing upstream. The channel is the throttle; the spillway is the relief valve; the Fish Mouth is the intake.

The rule carved into the site

The maintenance principle is usually summarised in six characters: deep dredge the channel, keep the weir low. The instruction is precise. If the channel is not dredged deep enough, the intake loses capacity and the plain starves. If the weir is built too high, it stops spilling and the flood protection fails. Both errors are easy to make and both are the kind of mistake that only shows up years later.

That is why the system has an annual maintenance cycle rather than a permanent structure. The river is allowed to silt during the flood season, then the silt is removed in the dry season, and the weir is rebuilt to its low profile each year using bamboo cages filled with stones. The technology is humble and the discipline is the achievement.

What the water built

The irrigated plain became known as the Land of Abundance. Reliable water meant two crops instead of one, which meant surplus, which meant tax revenue, markets and a population that could support crafts. Chengdu grew as the administrative and commercial centre of that surplus, and its wealth was agricultural before it was industrial.

UNESCO inscribed Mount Qingcheng and the Dujiangyan irrigation system in 2000, recognising the works as a living system rather than a ruin. The site is still in operation; the canals you see are the same channels, maintained in the same way, feeding the same plain.

Paper money, 1023

Sichuan's iron coinage was heavy and awkward, which is the classic condition for a substitute currency. Private paper notes began circulating in the region in the early eleventh century, and in 1023 the state took over the issue, creating what is generally described as the world's first government-issued paper currency. The notes were printed, serial-numbered, and backed by a reserve; the experiment later ran into the usual problem of over-issue, but the idea had been proved.

It is a strange pairing with the irrigation works — one manages water, the other manages trust — but both are systems for making a difficult environment predictable. Chengdu has specialised in that.

Brocade, and the name of the city

Shu brocade was a state-regulated luxury, woven here and sent north and west. The city was nicknamed the Brocade City after the official brocade workshops, and the craft is still demonstrated in the city. Silk and brocade fit the same pattern as grain: a surplus product that requires stable water, skilled labour and a market large enough to pay for quality.

Du Fu, and four years of thatch

Du Fu arrived in 759, during the An Lushan rebellion, and built a thatched cottage on the western edge of the city. He lived there for roughly four years and wrote a large body of poetry in that period, including the poem about the roof being torn off by wind. The cottage has been rebuilt many times and is now a memorial complex with a garden and a stream; what survives is not the building but the association.

The poems matter to the city's self-image because they establish a local type: the educated person who withdraws from political chaos to a small house with a view of water. That type recurs in Chengdu's teahouses, in its gardens, and in the way the city talks about its own pace.

The habit of the place

The basin climate is grey, humid and mild, with little wind. That is why the teahouses are full: bamboo chairs under a canopy, jasmine tea in a covered cup, and no expectation that anyone will leave soon. It is also why the local cuisine is heavy with chilli and Sichuan pepper — heat and numbness against a damp climate, though the explanation is cultural as much as meteorological.

Sanxingdui and Jinsha, the bronze-and-gold sites of a culture that vanished three millennia ago, sit at the other end of the story: a reminder that the plain had a sophisticated civilisation before the irrigation works, and that the archaeology of Sichuan keeps revising what "the beginning" means.
