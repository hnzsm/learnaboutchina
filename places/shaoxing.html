A City Engineered by Water, Then Rewritten as Fiction

A dam, a canal, a boat, a jar, a profession and a writer: the parts that made a plain habitable, and then made it famous.

Written and edited by one writer · Last reviewed November 2026 · Sources listed at the end

Shaoxing is usually explained as a water town, which puts the water in the wrong place in the sentence. The channels were not a setting for houses; they were why houses could stand there. A dam built in 140, a canal joining two river systems, a boat shaped to the channel, a wine that needed the water, a profession that exported paperwork and a writer who invented the place twice: those parts explain it. The scenery came afterwards.

Water: the street came second

The city stands at the northern foot of the Kuaiji Mountains, on the plain between the Qiantang and the Cao'e. Before it was drained, that plain was a marsh, flooded from the hills and open to the tide. The first construction here was not a street or a temple but a decision about where water could go.

In 490 BC Goujian, ruler of Yue, moved his capital here and had Fan Li lay out a small walled city on the south-east slope of Longshan. That is the date behind the claim to 2,500 years. The walls are gone; the address is not, and what made it workable was water management below.

The lake: a flood defence that became farmland

In 140 the governor of Kuaiji, Ma Zhen, built an embankment along the mountain front and impounded the streams behind it. The lake that formed, Jian Lake, was recorded as 358 li around, fed by thirty-six streams and irrigating over 9,000 qing. It held back floods and tide and stored water for dry seasons; the marsh became a granary.

Ma Zhen paid for it: landowners whose fields were flooded denounced him, and he died at Luoyang in disgrace. The work outlived him by eight centuries.

Then it was eaten. From the Northern Song, dykes were thrown up inside the lake and the bed was farmed; by the Southern Song most of it had gone, and what remained was managed as channels, not a reservoir. The lake today is a remnant of about 30 square kilometres. The plain still depends on sluices and pumps.

The canal: a road with no surface

The East Zhejiang Canal runs from Hangzhou through Shaoxing to Ningbo, linking the Qiantang to the sea. It carried grain, salt, timber, wine and people, and in 2014 it was inscribed with the Grand Canal as a World Heritage site.

Inside the city the channels were the streets. In the late Qing the walled city covered 7.4 square kilometres and held 37 kilometres of waterway and 229 stone bridges, a density Chen Congzhou's study put at twice Suzhou's and some 45 times that of pre-war Venice. Water was the road; bridges were the junctions.

The junctions are the architecture. Bazi Bridge, whose stone columns carry a rebuilding date of 1256, stands where three channels meet, with a main span, a subsidiary span at right angles and four flights of steps, so that two streets and three waterways cross without stopping. Markets gathered where boats could tie up.

The boat: a vehicle sized to the channel

The wupeng boat is what that system produced. The hull is narrow and pointed; the canopy is a low arch of woven bamboo, painted black with tung oil, in three to seven sections. The low roof is the design: it passes under bridges built low because the water beneath them was the road.

A small version, worked with a short oar over the stern and often called the foot-rowed boat, carries three or four people through lanes too narrow for a cart. The boat was not a picturesque accessory; it was why the canal worked as a street.

The wine: a water report in a jar

Shaoxing yellow rice wine is usually described by taste, which hides the causal chain. It is made from glutinous rice, wheat qu and water from Jian Lake. The water is soft, slightly alkaline and stable in mineral content, and brewers treat it as the fixed input while rice and qu vary.

The calendar matters as much as the recipe. Qu is made in autumn; the main brewing happens in winter, when low temperatures keep fermentation slow and the vats from overheating; the wine then ages in earthenware jars. The lake made the rice fields, the water went into the jar, and the canal carried the jars to market. The name became a guarantee: the technique was listed as national intangible heritage in 2006.

The secretary: paperwork as an export

In the Qing a county magistrate handled tax, policing, justice and correspondence, and could not do it alone. He hired private secretaries, and the legal and fiscal posts went disproportionately to men from Shaoxing prefecture. The saying was wu Shao bu cheng ya: without a Shaoxing man, no yamen.

The supply had a cause. Shaoxing produced more degree-holders than the bureaucracy could employ, and a failed candidate could learn a specialty: the Qing code, legal briefs, the arithmetic of the grain tax. Apprenticeship ran through native-place networks, so a magistrate who hired one Shaoxing secretary might hire his nephew next.

The export shaped the city. It produced men who spent their working lives in other provinces, sent money home and returned valuing exact procedure over moral display. The trade declined after the examination system was abolished in 1905, but the reputation outlasted the practice.

The writer: a hometown made of fiction

Lu Xun was born in 1881 in the Zhou family house on Dongchangfang, was sent to the Sanwei Study across the street at twelve, and left for Nanjing at eighteen. He published A Madman's Diary in 1918, Kong Yiji and Medicine in 1919, and Hometown in 1921.

Those stories are set in Lu Town, a fictional place assembled from Shaoxing: the wine shop where Kong Yiji stands at the counter because he cannot afford a seat, the canal from which boys row to a village opera, the courtyards where the New Year sacrifice is performed. Contemporaries identified Lu Town with the city at once, and the identification has stuck.

That is the interesting part for a city: a place can be given its self-portrait by a writer who left it and did not admire it. Lu Xun's Shaoxing is provincial and bound by custom, and the conservation area around his house preserves the buildings his stories use. Literature selected the heritage; the lanes no story needed are less protected.

The city: an address that stayed put

The administrative site has not moved for two and a half millennia. It was the Yue capital, then Kuaiji commandery, then Yue prefecture, then Shaoxing prefecture in 1131, named for a Southern Song reign title. The name changed with regimes; the ground did not.

The water system is the visible continuity: the old city is bounded by its ring canal and still holds eighteen channels, about 32 kilometres of waterway and 84 bridges, protected as a system, not as isolated monuments. A bridge without its channel is a sculpture. Put the pieces together and the place is an engineered plain, a transport network, a fermentation economy, an export trade in literacy and a literary landscape, stacked on one address.
