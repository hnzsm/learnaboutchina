The Capital That Moved Without Leaving the Valley

A river plain that held the seat of government for thirteen dynasties, and the reason its oldest walls are now hedgerows.

Written and edited by one writer · Last reviewed November 2026 · Sources listed at the end

Luoyang is introduced as a capital of thirteen dynasties, which is true and not very useful, because those dynasties did not build in the same place. Walled capitals were laid out along the Luo River over roughly two thousand years, each abandoned for a site a few kilometres away. Modern streets cover only the newest. The earlier layers survive as fields, and the monuments at either end of the valley explain why the sequence kept restarting.

The stack along the river

The valley's political centre moved east and west rather than up and down. Erlitou, near Yanshi, is the oldest large site. West of it lies the Han and Wei capital, and further west the Sui and Tang city that the present streets still follow. Each move answered something urgent: a flood, a sack, a dynasty that preferred a clean site to a ruined one.

The result is the opposite of Kaifeng, where old towns lie one beneath another because silt raised the ground with each rebuilding. At Luoyang the layers sit side by side, divided by farmland never built over. The earliest capitals survive as open ground rather than buried basements, so the archaeology reads as a change in the crop.

Erlitou and the problem of the Xia

The most important site was found late. A survey led by Xu Xusheng identified Erlitou in 1959, and excavation exposed a walled palace compound, a bronze foundry, a turquoise workshop and a road grid at a settlement that flourished from roughly 1900 to 1500 BC. This was a state-level centre: it cast ritual bronzes, organised specialist crafts and commanded labour.

Whether it was a Xia capital is a separate, contested question. Chinese archaeologists generally accept the identification; some scholars elsewhere are more cautious, because it rests on texts written centuries later rather than on writing from the site. The excavation shows a complex bronze-age polity. The name attached to it is an argument about documents.

A cliff turned into an institution

Longmen is a geological accident used with administrative discipline. For about a kilometre the Yi River cuts through limestone, and from the late fifth century that rock was carved: 2,345 niches and caves, more than 100,000 figures, some 2,800 inscriptions. Work began soon after the Northern Wei court moved its capital to Luoyang in 494 and continued through the Tang.

What makes the cliff readable is who paid. Some caves are imperial commissions, some are gifts of nobles, and many record donations by guilds and ordinary families. A wall of private gifts looks different from a wall of state projects, and the largest works are tied most tightly to political legitimacy. UNESCO inscribed the site in 2000 on criteria (i), (ii) and (iii).

Two bodies, two centuries apart

The comparison to make is between the Binyang Central Cave and the Fengxian Temple, carved for different courts, close enough to compare. Binyang Central was sponsored by the Northern Wei house and finished between 500 and 523. Its Buddha has an elongated face, high cheekbones, a faint archaic smile, and a robe cut as parallel incised folds that do not behave like cloth.

Fengxian was carved between 672 and 675 under the patronage of Empress Wu Zetian, and its Vairocana Buddha stands 17.14 metres high. The modelling differs in kind: a full face, shoulders with weight, drapery that falls like heavy fabric. These figures look like bodies that could stand up. The shift from linear abstraction to naturalistic mass marks the Sinicisation of Buddhist sculpture, and it happened on one bank within a century and a half.

The temple the state paid for

Buddhism reached China along trade routes long before the court, but the first temple the state built is the one that mattered institutionally. The White Horse Temple was founded in 68 AD, in the Eastern Han, after an imperial embassy returned from the west with two monks and scriptures carried, in the traditional account, on white horses.

The telling detail is the word for temple. Si was originally the name of a government office, including the one that received foreign visitors. The imported religion was housed in a building of that type, which shows how the Han court understood what it had acquired: a foreign cult under official supervision, not a popular movement. Almost nothing of the Han fabric remains; the continuity is in the site and the function.

A grid meant to be read from above

In 605 the Sui emperor Yang ordered a new eastern capital on the north bank of the Luo, and the histories say it was finished within about a year, which indicates the state's capacity for labour. The plan covered roughly 47 square kilometres, with the palace against the Mang hills, a ceremonial avenue running south, and the river crossed at the Tianjin Bridge.

The residential districts were divided into walled wards, 109 of them in the Tang counts, with three supervised markets. A ward was a block with gates shut at night; the markets kept their own hours under their own officials. This was an administrative city, built so that the population could be counted, taxed and watched. The grid was a system of walls, and the walls were the point.

What the canal gave, and what its rerouting took

The Sui canal system made Luoyang the centre of an imperial logistics network. The Tongji Canal ran southeast toward the Huai and the Yangtze, the Yongji Canal north toward the frontier, and southern grain could be stored in the city's granaries. An eastern capital where the tax system met the frontier lived on that position.

The advantage did not outlast its geography. The canals silted, dredging costs rose, and after the Tang collapse the capitals moved east, to Kaifeng and eventually Beijing. The Yuan rebuilt the Grand Canal through Shandong to feed the north, leaving Luoyang off the trunk route. A city does not need to be destroyed to be demoted. It only needs to stop being on the way.

The twentieth century added an administrative layer. The provincial capital went to Zhengzhou in 1954, but Luoyang received six Soviet-aided industrial projects under the First Five-Year Plan, and its tractor plant began construction in the western suburbs in 1955. The municipality holds about seven million people. The quiet belongs to the old core, not the city.

Why the old city looks empty

Three different things are confused in Luoyang. There is the modern municipality, large and industrial. There is the heritage corridor along the Luo, rebuilt as parks and reconstructed gates. And there is the buried city, protected mostly by being left alone. The open ground near the river is not neglect; it is the reason the earlier capitals remain intact enough to excavate.

Reconstruction raises the usual questions. A new gate on the Sui axis is an interpretation, candidly labelled, but it changes what a visitor means by old. The harder evidence lies under the fields and in the cliff: ward lines, bridge footings, a dated inscription at Fengxian. None of it has to be imagined, which is why the valley rewards attention to alignment before buildings.
