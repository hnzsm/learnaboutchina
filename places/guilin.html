A Landscape With Almost No Soil

Limestone, rainwater and a few hundred million years. The peaks are what is left when the rock between the cracks dissolves away.

Written and edited by one writer · Last reviewed November 2026 · Sources listed at the end

Guilin's scenery is the most reproduced in China: it is on banknotes, in textbooks, and on the wall of every Chinese restaurant that could afford a mural. The reason it looks like that is a single chemical reaction running for an unimaginably long time. Once you understand the reaction, the difference between a lone tower and a crowded cluster of peaks becomes obvious — and so does why the region is farmed the way it is.

Rock that rain can eat

The limestone here was laid down under a shallow tropical sea, mostly in the Devonian period, several hundred million years ago. It is made of calcium carbonate, much of it the compressed remains of marine organisms. When the sea withdrew and the beds were lifted, rain began to work on them.

Rainwater picks up carbon dioxide from the air and soil and becomes a weak acid. Calcium carbonate reacts with acid and dissolves, very slowly. On a landscape scale, over millions of years, that means whole bedding planes and fracture systems can be removed. What cannot be removed easily — the denser, less fractured blocks — remains standing.

This is the same process that makes caves, and it is worth holding both ideas at once: Guilin's towers and Guilin's caverns are the same phenomenon seen from outside and inside.

Why some peaks stand alone

Karst landscapes are usually divided into two forms. Where dissolution has gone far enough, the intervening rock is removed and you get isolated towers rising from a flat plain — in Chinese, fenglin, "peak forest". Where the process is less advanced, the peaks are still joined at the base and form a crowded cluster — fengcong, "peak cluster".

Guilin has both, often within sight of each other, which is why the skyline changes as you travel. The isolated towers around Yangshuo are the more mature version; the clustered ridges closer to Guilin are younger in the sense that less rock has been removed. Guangxi as a whole has more than 1,200 mapped karst peaks.

The shape of a tower also tells you its age. Young karst is rounded, because water works on the top surface. Old karst becomes steep-sided and conical, because the sides are what survive longest once the top has been lowered.

The river is the road

The Li River runs about 83 km from Guilin to Yangshuo, and the section between them is the classic route. It is not a scenic detour; it was the transport corridor for the region before there were roads, and the towns along it grew where boats could stop.

The bend near Xingping is the view printed on the back of the ¥20 note. The engraving has been there since the 1999 series, chosen so that the scenery would be recognisable to anyone handling the money. Standing at the viewpoint, you can line the note up with the actual peaks — a small, strange pleasure that also tells you how deliberately this landscape was chosen as a national image.

Inside the same rock

Reed Flute Cave is a dissolved chamber in the same limestone, with a walkway through it and coloured lighting on the formations. The lighting is theatrical, and it is easy to mock, but the cave itself is the more interesting object: stalactites growing down from the ceiling, stalagmites building up from the floor, and flowstone where water has run across a surface for long enough to deposit calcite.

If you have seen the towers, the cave completes the picture. The towers are the rock that survived dissolution at the surface; the cave is the rock that was removed underground. Same acid, same rock, different result depending on where the water goes.

Farming a vertical landscape

Flat ground is scarce, so the valley floors are used intensively: rice where water can be held, vegetables and citrus on the lower slopes, and ponds where the ground is too wet to build on. Water is managed by hand-dug channels and small weirs, often maintained by the same families for generations.

The result is a landscape that looks wild from a distance and is actually almost entirely worked. The bamboo rafts on the river are a tourist business now, but they are also the direct descendant of the boats that moved people and goods along the same water a century ago.

The terraces at Longji

About 100 km north of Guilin, in Longsheng county, the Longji terraces climb hillsides in long ribbons that follow the contour lines. They were cut over roughly seven centuries by Zhuang and Yao farmers, and they are still farmed — rice in the lower paddies, and dry crops higher up where the water will not hold.

The name means "Dragon's Backbone", after the way the ridges look from above. What makes them worth the detour is not the view but the labour: every one of those curves is a wall built by hand, on a slope steep enough that a single field may be only a few metres wide.

What tourism changed

Guilin was one of the first places in China to be promoted to foreign visitors after the country reopened in the late 1970s, and Yangshuo became a backpacker town in the 1980s. That history is visible in the economy: hotels, climbing shops, cooking schools and raft operators now outnumber the old river traffic.

It has also changed the villages. Yangshuo's old street is largely given over to guesthouses and souvenir shops, and the quieter corners are further out along the Yulong River. None of this is a scandal — the money is real and the landscape is protected — but it is worth knowing that the postcard image is now the product, not just the place.
