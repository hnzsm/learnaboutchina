Pillars Where Hills Should Be

Around 3,000 columns of quartz sandstone, cut by water along vertical cracks. The geology explains the skyline, and the engineering explains why you can reach it.

Written and edited by one writer · Last reviewed November 2026 · Sources listed at the end

Zhangjiajie is usually introduced through a film, which is a shame, because the actual explanation is better. The pillars are not limestone towers like those at Guilin. They are sandstone, which is a harder, more brittle rock, and that difference is why the landscape looks like a forest of columns instead of a cluster of rounded cones. Once you know what to look for, the park stops being scenery and becomes a lesson in how rock fails.

The wrong rock, the same result

Karst landscapes form when limestone dissolves. Water removes the rock chemically, opening caves and leaving towers behind. Zhangjiajie's sandstone does not dissolve that way; it is mostly quartz grains cemented together, and the process here is mechanical instead. The rock was laid down as sediment, compacted, then lifted and cracked.

Those cracks are the key. Sandstone beds here are cut by near-vertical joints, roughly parallel sets of fractures, and water gets into them. Freezing and thawing in winter, plus the simple force of water running down a crack, widen the joints gradually. Where the joints are close together, the intervening blocks narrow. Where they are far apart, wide blocks survive. The result is what you see: closely packed columns in some places, broad buttresses in others.

Wulingyuan, the UNESCO-listed area, covers about 264 km² and contains roughly 3,000 such pillars, some more than 100 m tall. They are not evenly spaced because the joint pattern was never even.

What the "park" actually is

Zhangjiajie is a city, a national forest park, and a much larger scenic area, and the names overlap confusingly. The core is made up of several connected parts — Zhangjiajie National Forest Park, Suoxiyu, Tianzi Mountain and Yangjiajie — which share one ticket system and one internal bus network. Visitors often think they are moving between separate attractions when they are moving between valleys of the same massif.

That matters for planning your day, but it also matters for understanding the place: the landscape is continuous, and the viewpoints are just places where the road or path happens to reach the rim.

The elevator built into the cliff

The Bailong elevator opened in 2002, rises about 326 m up a cliff face, and is usually described as the tallest outdoor lift in the world. It was built to replace a long climb, and it changed how the park works: what used to be a day's effort became a two-minute ride, and the volume of visitors at the top increased accordingly.

It is also the most contested piece of infrastructure in the park. Critics argued that a glass-and-steel shaft bolted to a sandstone cliff compromises the very thing people come to see. Supporters point out that without it, the numbers would simply climb the old path instead, with their own impact. Both are right, which is why the argument never quite ends.

Tianmen: a separate mountain, a separate problem

Tianmen Mountain is often folded into a Zhangjiajie trip, but it is a different ridge with a different geology — limestone, not sandstone — and a different gimmick. The "Heaven's Gate" is a natural arch about 131 m high punched through the ridge, reached by a flight of steps and, more commonly now, by a cableway roughly 7.5 km long that runs from the city edge up to the summit.

The arch is a collapsed cave. That is the whole explanation, and it is a good one: the same dissolution that makes Guilin's towers makes holes in mountains, and sometimes the roof of the hole falls in.

The glass bridge, and the economics of a view

In 2016 a glass-bottomed bridge opened over the Zhangjiajie Grand Canyon, about 430 m long and roughly 300 m above the canyon floor. It is not in the national forest park and it is not a through-route; it is a destination built to be photographed, and it works.

It is worth mentioning because it is the clearest example of a pattern across Chinese scenic areas: a natural feature becomes famous, visitor numbers rise, and then infrastructure is added to make the feature accessible and shareable. Some of that infrastructure is genuinely useful. Some of it is scenery about scenery.

Who was already here

The valleys inside the park boundary were farmed long before the boundary existed. Tujia and Miao communities, along with Bai and other groups, have villages in and around the massif, and the UNESCO listing explicitly covers the settlements as part of the cultural landscape rather than treating them as an intrusion.

This is easy to miss if you stay on the shuttle buses and the rim viewpoints. The park is not empty wilderness; it is a populated mountain that was designated for protection, and the people who farm it are part of what is being protected.

The film, and the renaming

After the 2010 film Avatar used similar floating formations, one pillar in the park was officially renamed for the film's mountain. In Chinese, the formations were and are simply "the columns" — a description, not a brand.

The renaming tells you something about how scenic places compete for attention, and it is worth noticing that the geological explanation is more remarkable than the film reference: a plateau, cracked along old joints, eroded from the cracks outward until what remains is a forest of stone.
