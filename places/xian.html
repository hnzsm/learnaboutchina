An Army Made of Clay

An emperor's garrison buried facing the states he had conquered, a capital laid out on a grid, and a wall whose corners were designed for crossfire.

Written and edited by one writer · Last reviewed November 2026 · Sources listed at the end

Xi'an is unusual among Chinese cities in that its two most famous monuments belong to different millennia and answer different questions. The Terracotta Army is about one man's attempt to take his empire into death. The city wall and the old grid are about how a capital was governed. Most itineraries treat them as a single stop; they are better understood as two layers of the same place, separated by eighteen hundred years.

The pit the farmers found

In 1974, farmers digging a well near Lintong hit a head made of terracotta. What they had found was the first of several pits dug into the plain around the unexcavated mound of Qin Shi Huang's tomb. Pit 1 is the long one you see in every photograph: corridors of packed earth walls, a timber roof that collapsed or was burned soon after burial, and ranks of soldiers standing in the positions they were assigned.

The figures are close to life size, roughly 1.8 to 2 metres, and the estimated total across the pits is about 8,000. They are not identical. Heads were modelled separately, so faces vary by rank, region and, in the case of a few, expression. Bodies and legs came from a smaller set of moulds and were assembled — an early production line, with variation applied at the end rather than built in from the start.

Why they face east

The army is drawn up facing east, out from the tomb, toward the states Qin had defeated to unify China in 221 BC. The arrangement is military, not ceremonial: infantry in the front corridors, archers and crossbowmen behind them, cavalry and chariots at the flanks, commanders in separate pits. It is a garrison on campaign, stationed for eternity.

The tomb itself has never been fully excavated. The Han historian Sima Qian, writing about a century later, described a chamber with rivers of mercury under a ceiling painted with stars — a claim modern surveys have partly supported by detecting unusual mercury concentrations in the soil. Excavating it would mean opening a sealed environment that has held for over two thousand years, which is why the mound is still a mound.

The bronze that stayed bright

Some weapons recovered from the pits were still sharp enough to cut, which is remarkable for bronze buried that long. Researchers reported traces of a chromium-rich coating on some blades. Whether that was a deliberate anti-rust treatment or an accidental result of the lacquer and soil around them is still argued in the literature, and it is worth being suspicious of anyone who states it as settled. What is not in doubt is that the weapons were real, standardised, and issued in quantity.

Chang'an: the city under the city

Xi'an was Chang'an, and under the Tang it was one of the largest cities in the world, with estimates around a million people. It was laid out as a rectangle of walled wards — around 108 of them — with two markets, an eastern one for officials and a western one for trade. The grid was not decorative. It let the state count, tax and police the population block by block, and it is the reason the modern city still has straight north–south streets in the old quarter.

Chang'an was also the eastern terminus of the Silk Road. Caravans arrived with horses, jade, glassware and metals, and left with silk, ceramics and tea. The city's foreign quarters were large enough to support their own places of worship, which is why a mosque has stood here since the eighth century.

The wall, and why it has 98 bastions

The city wall you can walk today is Ming work, built from 1370 onward on Tang foundations. It runs about 13.7 km around a rectangle, with a gate in the middle of each side and a moat outside. The detail that explains its shape is the row of projecting platforms — around 98 of them — spaced along the outside face.

A flat wall has a blind spot at its foot. A projecting platform lets defenders shoot along the wall face rather than straight out from it, so the intervals were sized for crossbow range. From above, the wall reads as a set of teeth: that is what the teeth are for.

A mosque that looks like a temple

The Great Mosque was founded in 742, during the Tang, for the Muslim traders and soldiers settled in the city, and it has been rebuilt several times since. It does not look like a mosque from the street. You enter through a Chinese garden courtyard, and the buildings are timber halls with tiled roofs; the call to prayer would have come from a pavilion built like a watchtower rather than a minaret. The inscriptions inside are in Chinese, not Arabic.

This is what religious architecture looks like when it grows in a foreign context for a thousand years: the function stays, the form absorbs the local grammar. It is a more useful thing to see than another hall of identical clay soldiers.

Huashan, and the limits of a vertical mountain

An hour or two east of the city, Huashan is a granite massif with a south peak at about 2,155 m and a reputation built on the path up. Sections of the route were cut as plank walkways bolted to the cliff face, and pilgrims have been using them for centuries. It is one of the five sacred Taoist mountains, and it is genuinely exposed — the mountain is not a theme park, and the weather turns quickly.

If you only have time for one thing outside the city, this is the one that changes how you think about Chinese landscape: vertical rock, narrow passes, and shrines built where there was no obvious place to build.
